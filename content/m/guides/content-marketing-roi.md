Content marketing ROI is the profit your content produced compared with what it cost you, usually written as a percentage: (revenue from content − cost of content) ÷ cost of content × 100. The formula takes ten seconds. The hard part is filling it in honestly, especially for videos and social posts, where the sale often happens later, somewhere else, and after several pieces of content.

This guide walks through the formula, the costs people forget, and a practical way to connect content on different platforms to the revenue it helped create.

## The content marketing ROI formula

The standard formula is:

```
Content ROI (%) = (Revenue from content − Cost of content) ÷ Cost of content × 100
```

For example, if a set of videos cost $2,000 to produce and promote, and they brought in $6,000 in sales, the ROI is ($6,000 − $2,000) ÷ $2,000 × 100 = 200%.

Some people prefer a simple revenue-to-cost ratio instead, such as "3 to 1". Both say the same thing in different ways. What matters is that you pick one and use it consistently.

## Step 1: Decide what counts as a return

Revenue is the cleanest return, but many content pieces end in a lead or a call and not an instant purchase. Decide in advance how you will value each outcome:

| Outcome | How to put a value on it |
| --- | --- |
| Purchase | The actual revenue from the order |
| Booked call or consultation | Your close rate × your average sale (for example, a 20% close rate on a $1,000 sale is worth about $200 per booked call) |
| Lead or newsletter signup | Only value it in dollars if you know what a subscriber is worth; otherwise report it separately as a leading indicator |
| Followers, views, likes | Not revenue. Track them, but keep them out of the ROI formula |

The dollar figures above are illustrations. Use your own numbers, and do not invent values you cannot defend.

## Step 2: Count the real cost

Most content ROI calculations look better than they should because the cost side is incomplete. Include all of these:

- **Production:** filming, editing, writing, design, equipment, freelancers or agency fees.
- **Tools:** software subscriptions you use to make, schedule or measure the content.
- **Distribution:** paid promotion, boosted posts or sponsored placements.
- **Your own time:** if you or your team make the content, that time is a cost. Give it a reasonable hourly value.

Leaving out your own time is the most common mistake. It makes nearly every content program look profitable.

## Step 3: Connect revenue to the content that earned it

This is where most measurement breaks. Platform analytics tell you about views, watch time and engagement. They do not tell you who later bought something on your website. To put revenue on the right side of the formula, you need to record three things:

1. Which piece of content a visitor came from.
2. What that visitor did next, such as signing up, booking a call or paying.
3. How much that outcome was worth.

Calculate ROI at two levels:

- **By channel,** such as all YouTube content versus all newsletter content, to decide where to spend time.
- **By piece,** such as one video versus another, to learn which topics and formats work.

## Step 4: Allow for content that keeps working

A video can keep sending viewers for months after you publish it. A fair ROI calculation has to allow for that:

- Pick a fixed measurement window, such as 30, 60 or 90 days after publishing, and use the same window for everything you compare.
- Compare content of a similar age. A video from last week and a video from six months ago are not comparable.
- Review older content again. A piece that looked weak early on may be earning steadily.

## Step 5: Share the credit when several pieces helped

Many customers see more than one piece of content before they buy. If you give all the revenue to the last link they clicked, you will overvalue the content that closes sales and undervalue the content that started the journey. The [multi-touch attribution guide](/m/guides/multi-touch-attribution/) explains the credit models in detail.

Here is a small hypothetical example. A customer watches Video A, then Video B, then Video C, and then buys a $1,000 offer. Each video cost $300 to produce.

| Method | Video A | Video B | Video C |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Last-touch (all credit to C) | $0 revenue, ROI −100% | $0 revenue, ROI −100% | $1,000 revenue, ROI 233% |
| Equal credit (about $333 each) | ROI about 11% | ROI about 11% | ROI about 11% |

Under last-touch, you would cut Videos A and B. Under equal credit, all three look slightly profitable, and cutting A and B might remove the start of the journey. Neither method is the truth, but comparing both shows you where the decision is sensitive.

## Common content ROI mistakes

- **Counting only ad spend as cost.** Production and time are costs too.
- **Treating views or followers as the return.** Attention is not revenue.
- **Judging content too early.** Content that works over months needs a longer window.
- **Comparing unequal windows.** Always compare pieces over the same period.
- **Missing untracked sharing.** Private shares and word of mouth are invisible to most tools, so your ROI is probably understated. The [dark social guide](/m/guides/dark-social-tracking/) covers how to measure more of it.
- **Treating the number as exact.** Content ROI is an estimate that guides decisions. It is not an audit.

## What to do with your ROI numbers

- **Spend more where ROI is clearly positive,** and test whether the result holds as you scale.
- **Fix the next step when clicks are high but revenue is low.** The problem may be the offer or the page, not the content.
- **Look at pieces that start journeys.** Content with weak last-touch results may still be doing important discovery work.
- **Stop or rework formats that never pay back** after a fair measurement window.
- **Repeat the review on a schedule,** so you compare like with like over time.

## Content marketing ROI FAQ

### What is a good content marketing ROI?

There is no universal answer. A good ROI depends on your profit margins, how long your content takes to pay back, and what else you could do with the same money and time. A useful comparison is your own other channels, not a number from an industry report.

### How do I calculate ROI for social media content?

Use the same formula. Add up everything you spent on making, promoting and measuring the content, including your time. Then use the revenue you can connect to that content through tracking links and recorded outcomes.

### Can I measure ROI for free content like YouTube videos?

Yes. Free to publish does not mean free to make. Count production, tools and your time as cost. For return, count the revenue you can tie to the videos. Platform payouts such as ad revenue also count if you earn them.

### How long should I wait before measuring content ROI?

Choose a fixed window, such as 30, 60 or 90 days, and apply it to every piece. The right length depends on how long your customers take to decide.
