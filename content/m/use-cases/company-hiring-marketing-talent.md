# How to Hire Your First Marketing Employee

The best way to hire your first marketing employee is to decide what single outcome the role must produce, match the candidate's skills to that outcome, and judge candidates with structured questions and a real work exercise instead of a résumé and a good conversation. You don't need to be a marketer to do this. You need a clear definition of the job, a scorecard written before you meet anyone, and a way to see candidates do work that resembles the work you are hiring for.

This guide is written for founders, owners, and hiring managers who are about to make that first marketing hire and can't easily judge the work themselves. It covers what the role should actually be responsible for, which skills matter, how to evaluate candidates when you aren't an expert, the mistakes that most often go wrong, and how to test practical ability, including by giving finalists the same real product and comparing measurable outcomes.

## What your first marketing hire is actually for

A first marketing hire is not a marketing department. It is one person, working with a limited budget, on a business that usually doesn't yet know which channels work. That changes what you should hire for.

Before you write a job description, answer three questions in plain language:

1. **What outcome should this person move in the first six months?** Qualified leads, trial sign-ups, booked sales calls, online sales, or something else. "Grow our brand" is not an outcome you can evaluate.
2. **Where is the business stuck?** You may have a product people like but no way to reach them, traffic that doesn't convert, or demand you can't capture because nobody owns the website and messaging.
3. **Which channel is most likely to work for your customers?** Search, social content, email, partnerships, paid ads, events. You don't need to be certain. You need a hypothesis the hire can test.

The answers determine the kind of marketer you need. The table below is practical guidance rather than research, but it shows how the problem shapes the profile.

| If your main problem is... | Look first for... |
|---|---|
| Nobody knows you exist and you sell to people who spend time on social platforms | A content or social-led marketer who can create and publish consistently |
| People visit but don't sign up or buy | A marketer with strong messaging, landing page, and conversion experience |
| You have a list of leads but they go cold | A lifecycle or email marketer who can build follow-up |
| You have a budget and want faster tests | A performance marketer, but only if tracking is already in place |
| Several of the above, with no budget to split them | A generalist who has run a small company's marketing end to end |

## What a first marketing hire should be responsible for

Keep the first hire's scope narrow enough that you can tell whether it worked. A reasonable scope usually includes:

- **Owning one or two channels end to end.** Planning, creating, publishing, and improving, not just advising.
- **Setting up basic measurement.** Every campaign should have a way to connect activity to sign-ups or sales. If your first hire can't explain how they will know what worked, that is a warning sign.
- **Shipping on a regular rhythm.** Weekly output you can see matters more than a polished strategy deck.
- **Learning the customer's language.** Talking to customers, reading support requests, and turning what they hear into messaging.
- **Reporting honestly.** Including what didn't work and what they would change.

Things that usually don't belong in a first hire's scope: running five channels at once, building a brand identity from scratch, managing agencies, handling public relations, and doing the data engineering for your analytics stack. Each is a real job. Piling them onto one person is how a good hire fails.

## The skills that matter, and how they show up

Marketing is a wide field, so "good at marketing" tells you very little. These are the capabilities that tend to matter most in a first hire. Each one can be observed, which is the point.

| Skill | What it looks like in practice |
|---|---|
| Positioning and messaging | Explains who the product is for and why, in plain words, and can rewrite your pitch for a specific audience |
| Channel craft | Knows how the chosen channel works: what to publish, how often, how to tell if it's working |
| Execution speed | Produces real work on a schedule and doesn't wait for perfect conditions |
| Measurement literacy | Can describe what they measure, what the numbers can't tell them, and how they separate their effect from everything else going on |
| Experimentation | Treats ideas as tests, with a hypothesis and a stopping point |
| Communication and ownership | Shares progress without being chased and takes responsibility for results, including misses |

Notice that "creativity" and "team fit" don't appear as separate numbers. They matter, but they are the hardest to score, so you should evaluate them directly by looking at the work and talking to the person, and treat them as judgment calls rather than metrics.

## Why résumés, interviews, and portfolios mislead you

If you aren't a marketer, the three standard signals are especially unreliable.

**Résumés describe claims, and the context is invisible.** "Grew revenue 3x" depends on the budget, the product, the market, the team around the person, and the platform conditions at the time. You usually can't separate the individual's contribution from all of that, and tactics that worked on last year's platforms may not work today.

**Portfolios show output, not outcomes.** A strong-looking campaign tells you the person can produce good-looking work. It doesn't tell you whether it moved a number, or how much of it the candidate did alone.

**Unstructured interviews reward fluency.** Marketers talk about marketing for a living. The most articulate person in the room and the person who would do the best work on your product are not always the same.

The research on hiring points the same way. A major 2022 re-analysis of decades of studies in the *Journal of Applied Psychology* found that structured interviews, which use the same questions and scoring standards for every candidate, ranked as the strongest predictor of job performance among commonly used selection methods ([Sackett et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000994)). The estimates in that re-analysis were considerably lower than earlier ones, which is a useful reminder that no single method is a guarantee. A U.S. Department of the Interior [assessment guide for hiring managers](https://www.doi.gov/sites/default/files/assessment-cheat-sheet-for-hiring-managers.pdf) summarizes the revised figures: structured interviews at about .42 and work samples at about .33, while years of experience sits near the bottom at about .07. Those are averages across many kinds of jobs, not marketing specifically, and they are not predictions for any one hire. The practical takeaway is modest but useful: structure your interviews, use real work where you can, and don't treat years of experience as a stand-in for ability.

## How to evaluate candidates when you're not a marketing expert

### 1. Write the scorecard before you meet anyone

Decide what you will evaluate and how, and write it down. A workable scorecard has the outcome the role owns, four to six criteria (for example: messaging, channel craft, measurement, execution, communication), and a short description of what a weak, solid, and strong answer looks like for each. Scoring against criteria you set in advance protects you from being swayed by whoever interviews most smoothly.

### 2. Use a structured interview

The U.S. Office of Personnel Management describes a structured interview as one that asks candidates about past behavior or how they would respond to job-related situations, using the same questions and rating standards for everyone ([OPM](https://opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/assessment-and-selection/other-assessment-methods/structured-interviews)). In practice that means writing your questions ahead of time, asking them in the same order, and scoring each answer against the rubric before you compare notes.

Questions that work well for a first marketing hire:

- *"Walk me through one campaign you ran. What was the starting point, what did you change, what happened, and how do you know it was your work that caused it?"* A strong answer names a baseline, a specific action, a result, and the limits of what they can claim.
- *"Tell me about something you tried that didn't work. What did you do next?"* Look for a concrete example and a lesson, not a disguised success story.
- *"If you had six months and this budget, what would you test first, and why?"* Look for a hypothesis, a way to measure it, and an honest sense of the trade-offs.
- *"How would you know after 30 days whether this is working?"* Strong candidates talk about leading indicators and are honest that some results take longer.
- *"What would you need from me in the first two weeks?"* Good candidates ask about customers, past data, and access, not only about budget.
- *"Which parts of this role would you want help with?"* Self-awareness is a useful signal in a first hire.

Probe for specifics. Vague answers ("we increased engagement") are normal. Follow-ups like "what was it before?" and "what did you personally do?" separate the candidate who did the work from the one who was nearby when it happened.

### 3. Check references on the candidate's own contribution

Ask references what the candidate personally owned, what the results were, and what they would want a new manager to know. A reference who can only describe the team's results is giving you less information than one who can describe the candidate's.

### 4. Get a second opinion on finalists

If budget allows, pay an experienced marketer for an hour or two to review your finalists' work and answers. It's a small cost compared with a wrong hire. SHRM-cited estimates put the cost of replacing an employee at roughly half to twice annual salary, depending on the role ([SHRM](https://www.shrm.org/mena/ar/labs/resources/when-candidates-can-fake-skills-real-time-assessment-design)). That figure covers replacement broadly rather than marketing hires specifically, but the direction is clear: a bit of extra evaluation is cheap by comparison.

### 5. Add a practical exercise

Conversation tells you how someone talks about work. A practical exercise shows you some of the work itself. That is the subject of the next section.

## Assessing practical ability: work samples and trial projects

Common ways to see real work before you hire:

- **A short take-home task.** A few hours on a focused problem, such as auditing your landing page, drafting a first-month plan, or rewriting a piece of messaging.
- **A live working session.** Review your actual numbers together and see how the candidate reasons.
- **A paid trial project.** A bounded piece of real work, paid at a fair rate.
- **A real-product challenge.** Finalists promote the same product and you compare measurable outcomes (covered below).

A few design rules keep these fair and useful:

- **Make it representative.** Test what the role will actually do. Don't evaluate a content marketer on paid media.
- **Give everyone the same brief and the same rules,** and tell candidates in advance what you will evaluate.
- **Keep it proportionate.** The more work you ask for, the more you should pay for it. A long unpaid exercise that produces assets you will use is the kind of arrangement that deserves legal attention.
- **Settle ownership up front.** Be explicit about who owns anything a candidate creates and whether you may use it.
- **Check the rules where you operate.** In the United States, the Department of Labor's [guidance on unpaid internships](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/71-flsa-internships) generally cautions against using unpaid placements as a trial period for people seeking employment, and compensation rules differ by country and by state. If a task benefits your business, get advice from an employment lawyer before asking people to do it for free.

## Testing Candidates on the Same Real Product, With Measurable Outcomes

For some marketing roles, especially those where the job is creating and distributing content or promoting an offer, there is a more direct way to see how people work: give each finalist the same real product and compare what they produce and what it generates.

The basic structure:

1. **Shortlist first.** A challenge asks candidates for real effort, so use it after a structured interview or skills screen, and only with a small group of finalists.
2. **Choose a real product, offer, or page** you want promoted.
3. **Write one short brief** that every candidate receives, including the objective, timeframe, and what you will evaluate.
4. **Give each candidate their own trackable link** to the same destination, so each person's activity can be separated.
5. **Let them promote it their own way** on the platforms they choose.
6. **Compare the evidence side by side:** their content, the engagement the platforms report, and the clicks, conversions, and revenue that can be attributed to each person.
7. **Talk to your top candidates** about the ideas behind the numbers before deciding.

The idea behind it is the same one behind any work sample: you learn more from what people do than from what they say they would do.

### What this method can and can't tell you

It is evidence to weigh, not a verdict. Be clear-eyed about its limits:

- **Platforms don't all provide the same data.** A tracking tool can only report the activity and performance data that connected platforms make available, and what is available differs from one platform to the next. Don't compare a metric on one platform with a different metric on another as if they were the same.
- **Attribution is never perfect.** No tracking method sees everything. Some people watch a video, switch devices, or buy days later, and some never click at all.
- **Audience size, algorithms, content quality, timing, and luck all affect results.** A candidate with an existing following has a head start, a platform's algorithm may favor one post over another, and a single post that happens to do well can make someone look better than they are. Decide in advance how you will account for these, and look at results over time, not just on day one.
- **Revenue alone doesn't decide who the best marketer is.** Creativity, judgment, communication, and team fit don't show up as numbers. Combine the results with your interviews and your read on how the person works.
- **It suits some roles better than others.** It fits roles built around creating and distributing content or promoting offers. It is a weaker test for roles centered on analytics, internal coordination, or long-cycle strategy, where a structured interview and a different work sample will serve you better.
- **It doesn't guarantee a good hire.** It gives you more to base the decision on than a résumé and an interview.

### Running it fairly: shortlist, pay, ownership, and the law

- **Shortlist before you ask for real effort.** The 100-applicant scenario shows the idea at scale, but in practice screen first with a skills test, a short task, or a structured interview, then invite a few finalists into the challenge. It respects candidates' time and gives you results you can actually review.
- **Pay fairly for the work.** The more time a challenge takes and the more it benefits your business, the more you should compensate candidates. In the United States, the Department of Labor's [guidance on unpaid internships](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/71-flsa-internships) generally cautions against using unpaid placements as a trial period for people seeking employment, and rules differ by country and state.
- **Settle content ownership in writing.** Say who owns what candidates create, whether you may reuse it, and whether candidates may keep it on their own channels.
- **Check employment law where you operate.** How a trial arrangement is classified, and what you owe participants, varies. Get advice from an employment lawyer before you launch.
- **Use the same brief and rules for everyone,** and tell candidates in advance what you will evaluate.

## Common mistakes when hiring a first marketer

1. **Hiring without a defined outcome.** If you can't say what success looks like in six months, you can't evaluate the hire, and neither can they.
2. **Hiring for the wrong channel.** A great paid-ads specialist can't fix a messaging problem, and a great writer can't fix broken tracking.
3. **Choosing a specialist when you need a generalist, or the reverse.** One person is unlikely to be excellent at everything. Decide where depth matters.
4. **Over-weighting a famous employer.** Results at a large company with a big brand and big budget often don't transfer to a small business with neither.
5. **Being swayed by interview charisma.** Structure and a scorecard are the antidote.
6. **Not having measurement in place.** If nothing tracks sign-ups or sales by source, nobody can tell whether the hire is working, including the hire.
7. **Expecting instant results.** Some channels take months to build. Agree on leading indicators for 30, 60, and 90 days instead of a single revenue number.
8. **Asking for large amounts of free work.** It's unfair to candidates and can create legal and reputational problems.

## Setting the hire up to succeed

The hiring decision is only half of it. A good first marketer can still fail if they start without access to customers, data, or a clear owner on your side. In the first weeks, give them access to your product, your customers, and any past numbers; agree on the one or two channels and the measurement approach; set a weekly review where they show work and results; and decide in advance what you will look at after 30, 60, and 90 days.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should I look for in my first marketing hire?

Look for evidence they can own one outcome end to end: clear thinking about your customer, strong execution in the channel you need, an honest approach to measurement, and good communication. Specific, checkable examples of their own work matter more than titles or years of experience.

### Should my first marketing hire be a generalist or a specialist?

With a small budget and several open problems, a generalist who has run a small company's marketing is usually more useful. If your problem is concentrated in one area, such as converting existing traffic or running paid campaigns on a working funnel, a specialist can make sense. Decide based on where your bottleneck is.

### How do I hire a marketer if I'm not a marketer?

Define the outcome, write a scorecard, run structured interviews with the same questions for everyone, check references on each candidate's own contribution, and add a practical exercise. If you can, pay an experienced marketer to review your finalists.

### Should I hire an employee, a freelancer, or an agency first?

Each trades off cost, commitment, and control. A freelancer or agency lets you test with less commitment, while an employee builds knowledge inside the company and is easier to direct day to day. Whichever you choose, define the outcome and the measurement first.

### Is it legal to ask candidates for unpaid work?

It depends on where you are and what you ask for. In the United States, Department of Labor guidance on unpaid internships generally cautions against using unpaid placements as a trial period for people seeking employment, and other countries and states have their own rules. If a task benefits your business, speak to an employment lawyer, or pay candidates for their time.

### How long until a new marketing hire shows results?

It depends on the channel and your sales cycle. Paid campaigns can produce signals quickly, while content and search take longer. Agree on leading indicators for the first 30, 60, and 90 days rather than promising a revenue number, and check that tracking is in place from day one.

### Can I compare candidates by having them promote the same product?

Yes, for roles built around content and promotion. Give each shortlisted candidate the same brief and an individual trackable link, set the rules and timeframe in advance, and compare results alongside the ideas behind them. The platforms don't all report the same data, attribution is never perfect, and audience size, timing, and luck all influence results, so treat the numbers as evidence to weigh together with your interviews and judgment. Handle payment and content ownership fairly.

### Which hiring assessment tool should I use for a marketing role?

It depends on the question you need answered. Skills-test tools such as TestGorilla or Workello help screen many applicants on knowledge and basic skills, a simulation tool such as Vervoe shows how candidates handle role-like tasks, and an applicant tracking system such as Workable can help you run structured interviews with scorecards. Check each vendor's current features and pricing before you decide.

### Is VSTRK a replacement for skills tests, interview scorecards, or an applicant tracking system?

No. VSTRK is a complementary method. It helps you observe promotional activity and attributable outcomes when shortlisted candidates promote the same product through individual tracking links. It doesn't test knowledge, structure your interviews, or manage applications.

## Conclusion

Hiring your first marketing employee is hard mainly because you are being asked to judge work you can't see yet. The fix isn't to become a marketing expert overnight. It is to define the outcome, write the scorecard first, interview with structure, check references on the candidate's own contribution, and look at real work before you commit.

No single method answers every question. Skills tests show knowledge, scorecards make interviews consistent, and simulations show how someone works through a task. For roles where the job is promoting a product through content, giving a few finalists the same real product and comparing what their promotion produces can add evidence the others can't.
